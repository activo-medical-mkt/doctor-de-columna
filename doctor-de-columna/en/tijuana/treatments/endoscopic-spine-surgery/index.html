[01] · What the surgery is and what it aims to do

What is
endoscopic spine surgery?

Lumbar endoscopy is a minimally invasive surgery that uses a camera to see the discs and nerve roots. The surgeon passes instruments through small openings to remove a herniated disc fragment or other tissue pressing on a nerve. The procedure depends on the cause and location of that pressure.

  • It can be used for herniated discs and some types of lumbar narrowing.
  • The technique is chosen based on your diagnosis and anatomy.
  • Not every spine problem needs surgery.

Reference: AAOS · Endoscopic discectomy.

Evaluation for endoscopic surgery
Understand your diagnosis

Conditions that may be treated
with lumbar endoscopy.

Endoscopy may be considered for some herniated discs and narrowing that compress lumbar nerves. Sciatica can be a sign of that compression; your diagnosis and exam determine whether there is a reason for surgery.

01
Spine condition

Lumbar disc herniation

Part of a disc can shift and press on a nerve root, causing pain down the leg, tingling or weakness. An endoscopic discectomy can remove the fragment responsible when surgery is indicated.

02
Spine condition

Lumbar spinal stenosis

Narrowing of the lumbar spinal canal can cause pain, numbness or difficulty walking. The evaluation looks at where the nerves are compressed and whether an endoscopic decompression can give them room without compromising stability.

03
Spine condition

Foraminal stenosis

The foramen is the opening a nerve root passes through as it leaves the spine. When it narrows, it can cause leg symptoms. In selected cases, a foraminotomy is considered to widen that opening.

04
Related symptom

Sciatica from nerve compression

Sciatica is pain that can travel through the buttock and leg when a nerve is irritated or compressed. Endoscopy treats certain causes of that compression, such as a herniated disc; having sciatica alone is not a reason for surgery.

Having a herniated disc, stenosis or sciatica doesn’t on its own mean you need endoscopic surgery.

Disc, canal and foramen

Lumbar endoscopy
procedures.

These procedures aim to free compressed nerves, but they work on different structures: the disc, the lumbar canal or the opening where a nerve root exits. They can be combined when the diagnosis calls for it.

01 · Disc

Endoscopic
discectomy

Removes the herniated part of a disc that is pressing on a nerve root; the whole disc is not removed. It’s considered when the location of the herniation and your symptoms justify the procedure.

02 · Nerve exit

Endoscopic
foraminotomy

Widens the foramen where a nerve root exits. It can be part of a decompression, and whether it’s indicated depends on what is narrowing that opening and on the stability of the lumbar segment.

03 · Lumbar canal

Endoscopic lumbar
decompression

Removes tissue narrowing the canal or the space around the nerves, such as part of a thickened ligament or bone that adds to the compression. How much is decompressed is planned from your imaging and exam.

Approaches and portals

Lumbar endoscopy techniques:
approaches and portals.

Transforaminal and interlaminar describe the route to the problem area. Uniportal and biportal describe how the openings for the camera and instruments are arranged. They are separate choices that can be combined.

Approach

Transforaminal
or interlaminar approach

The transforaminal approach goes through the foramen, where a nerve root exits. The interlaminar approach uses the space between the laminae of the vertebrae. The lumbar level, your anatomy and the location of the problem guide the choice.

How the openings are arranged

Uniportal
and biportal endoscopy

In uniportal endoscopy, the instruments pass through the endoscope’s working channel. In biportal endoscopy, the camera and instruments go in through separate openings. The proposed approach and the reasons for it are explained during your evaluation.

Technical reference: Neurospine · Indications and approaches in endoscopic spine surgery.

Preparation, discharge and follow-up

Preparing for and recovering
from lumbar endoscopy.

A lumbar endoscopy plan includes anesthesia preparation, treating the compression and follow-up afterward. Discharge and your return to work or exercise are tailored to the procedure and how you recover.

01

Preparation and anesthesia evaluation

The team reviews your tests, medical history and medications. Before surgery, they explain the type of anesthesia, fasting and what to do with your usual medications.

02

Treating the nerve compression

The camera shows the work area while the herniated fragment or tissue pressing on the nerve is removed. The surgical and anesthesia teams monitor you throughout the procedure.

03

Discharge, activity and follow-up visits

Discharge instructions cover wound care, pain control and allowed activities. At follow-up visits, your return to work, exercise and physical therapy is adjusted to how you’re recovering.

i

Risks and limitations of lumbar endoscopy.

Endoscopic surgery can lead to infection, bleeding, nerve injury or a tear in the dura, the covering that holds the fluid around the nerves. Pain may also persist or a herniation may come back. The technique doesn’t fix every cause of low back pain; your evaluation should explain your personal risks, the alternatives and the result that can reasonably be expected.

Endoscopic spine
surgery FAQs.

Answers about indications, anesthesia, hospital stay, recovery and costs for lumbar endoscopic surgery.

01

Can every herniated disc be treated with endoscopy?

No. The location and features of the herniation, the stability of your spine and your symptoms determine whether endoscopy is an option. Many herniated discs are managed without surgery. If surgery is considered, the appropriate alternatives for your diagnosis are compared.

02

Are endoscopy and microdiscectomy the same thing?

No. Both can be used to remove a herniated fragment pressing on a nerve, but they use different tools and approaches. Endoscopy uses a camera; microdiscectomy usually uses a microscope. The choice depends on the problem and the surgical evaluation.

03

Do I need an MRI before my consultation?

If you already have an MRI, bring the images and the report, along with any other tests you have. Dr. Cisneros will review them alongside your exam and decide whether you need more imaging; surgery is never recommended based on an image alone.

04

What kind of anesthesia is used?

General anesthesia may be used or, for certain procedures, local anesthesia with sedation. The plan depends on the technique, the expected length of the procedure and your health, and it’s explained during the anesthesia evaluation.

05

Can I go home the same day?

It’s possible for some procedures, but it isn’t guaranteed. The complexity of the surgery, your health and how you do right afterward may require observation or a hospital stay. The team confirms discharge and the instructions for leaving with a companion.

06

When can I go back to work or exercise?

There’s no single timeline for every patient. An office job and work that involves heavy lifting need different plans. Follow-up visits let us adjust your return to work, exercise and physical therapy to the procedure and how you’re recovering.

07

How much does endoscopic spine surgery cost?

The estimate is set once the procedure you need has been identified. Ask for a breakdown of the surgeon’s fees, hospital, anesthesia and equipment, what the quote includes and what might be billed separately. You can ask about the evaluation process at +52 664 159 6633.
