Spine surgery:
your options.

The technique is chosen based on your diagnosis, where the problem is and how stable your spine is. Here’s what each procedure aims to do and how they differ, so you can come to your visit with questions.

01 · Spine surgery

Endoscopic spine surgery

A camera and very small openings.

Endoscopy uses a camera to see the discs and nerve roots. It can be used to treat certain herniated discs and types of lumbar narrowing. Whether it’s an option depends on the problem and your anatomy.

  • Camera and instruments through small openings.
  • May include discectomy, decompression or foraminotomy.
  • Not every problem can be treated this way.
Learn about endoscopic spine surgery
02 · Spine surgery

Microdiscectomy

Removing the piece of disc pressing on a nerve.

Microdiscectomy uses magnification to remove herniated disc material that is pressing on a nerve root. It’s considered when your symptoms, exam and imaging all point to that herniation.

  • Targets compression caused by the disc.
  • The approach depends on where the herniation is.
  • Recovery and restrictions are tailored to you.
Learn about microdiscectomy
03 · Spine surgery

Lumbar decompression

Making room for the nerves in the canal or foramen.

Decompression frees the nerves by removing some of the tissue that narrows the lumbar canal or the opening where a nerve root exits. It can be done with different techniques, including endoscopy in selected cases.

  • Considered for certain cases of stenosis.
  • How much is done depends on where the compression is.
  • Spinal stability is checked when planning it.
Learn about lumbar decompression
04 · Spine surgery

Spinal fusion

Stabilizing a segment when it’s needed.

Spinal fusion joins two or more vertebrae to stabilize a segment of the spine. It may be considered when your diagnosis and spinal stability call for it; not every decompression needs a fusion.

  • The reason for it is explained based on your diagnosis.
  • Can be combined with decompression in some cases.
  • Your plan includes follow-up while the bone heals.
Learn about spinal fusion

Procedures can be combined, and some goals can be reached with more than one technique. Use this to prepare your questions; choosing the right one takes an individual evaluation.

Your diagnosis comes first

When is spine
surgery considered?

Persistent pain, weakness or trouble walking may call for a surgical evaluation. The decision weighs which problem explains your symptoms, what you’ve already tried and the benefits and risks of surgery.

Book a spine evaluation
  1. 01

    Matching symptoms and imaging

    Your exam, strength and sensation are compared with the imaging. An MRI finding alone is never a reason for surgery.

  2. 02

    Reviewing alternatives and past treatment

    We look at how you’ve responded to medication and physical therapy, along with the options that fit your diagnosis.

  3. 03

    Agreeing on goals and explaining the risks

    Your evaluation covers what surgery is meant to fix, its limits, possible complications and the follow-up you’d need.

01

How do I know if I need spine surgery?

You need an evaluation that matches your symptoms and exam with your imaging. Previous treatment, spinal stability and your overall health are also considered. An MRI showing a herniated disc doesn’t on its own mean you need surgery.

02

Are endoscopy and microdiscectomy the same thing?

They’re different techniques. Both can remove disc material pressing on a nerve; endoscopy uses a camera and microdiscectomy uses magnification. The technique is chosen based on the problem, your anatomy and the surgeon’s evaluation.

03

Does all spine surgery involve screws?

No. Some surgeries only remove a herniation or free a nerve, without a fusion. Whether stabilization is needed depends on your diagnosis, the stability of the segment and the planned procedure.

04

What are the risks of spine surgery?

Risks include infection, bleeding, nerve injury, a tear in the dura and symptoms that persist or come back. There are also anesthesia risks and, with a fusion, the bone may not heal as expected. Your visit should explain the specific risks and alternatives for your case.

05

How long is recovery?

It depends on the procedure, your health and how you heal. Discharge, physical therapy and your return to work or exercise are tailored to you; there’s no single timeline for every spine surgery.

06

What should I bring to my first visit?

Bring any imaging and reports you have, your medication list and details of previous treatment or surgery. The doctor will decide whether other tests are needed.

07
