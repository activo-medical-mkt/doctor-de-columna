What is
spondylolisthesis?

Spondylolisthesis happens when a vertebra slips forward over the one below it. It’s most common in the lower back, especially at L4–L5 and L5–S1, and it can narrow the space for the nerves or make that segment of the spine unstable.

  • Many people get better without surgery.
  • A mild slip may cause no symptoms.
  • Treatment depends on your symptoms, not just the grade.

Types and grades

Types of spondylolisthesis
by cause

Spondylolisthesis is classified by what causes the slip. In adults, the most common types are degenerative and isthmic.

01 · Wear and tear
L4–L5
Most common
after age 50.

Degenerative spondylolisthesis

Wear and tear on the discs and joints lets the vertebra slip. It often comes with spinal stenosis.

02 · From a stress fracture
L5–S1
Can start
at a young age.

Isthmic spondylolisthesis

A small fracture in the back part of the vertebra, called spondylolysis, allows it to slip.

03 · Other causes
Injury
Surgery
Less
common.

Other types

Injuries, previous spine surgery or conditions present from birth. These are assessed during your visit.

Spondylolisthesis grades by amount of slip

On an X-ray, the slip is measured by how far the vertebra has moved forward over the one below. The grade guides the diagnosis, but treatment is decided by your symptoms.

GradeHow far the vertebra slipsWhat it usually means
Grade IUp to 25% of its width.The most common grade, and often treated without surgery.
Grade IIBetween 25% and 50%.Needs follow-up, especially if there is persistent pain or leg symptoms.
Grades III to VMore than 50%; in grade V the vertebra has completely lost contact with the one below.These are uncommon and are usually evaluated for stabilization surgery.
What you may feel

Symptoms of
spondylolisthesis

Some people have no symptoms. When symptoms appear, they usually get worse when you stand, walk or lean back. Explore each one to learn more.

01

Lower
back pain

Lower back pain that gets worse with activity or standing for a long time and eases when you rest or sit.

02

Pain that travels
down the leg

If the slip narrows the space around a nerve, you may get pain into the buttock and leg, similar to sciatica.

03

Stiffness and
muscle tightness

Tightness in the lower back and the back of the thighs, with less flexibility when you bend over.

04

Tingling or
weakness

Tingling, numbness or weakness in the legs or feet needs to be evaluated. If it’s new or getting worse, seek prompt care and review the emergency warning signs on this page.

Differential diagnosis

Conditions that can mimic
spondylolisthesis

Spinal stenosis, herniated discs and sciatica can cause similar symptoms and sometimes occur along with the slip. An evaluation tells them apart.

01

Spinal stenosis

Often occurs with degenerative spondylolisthesis and causes discomfort when walking.

02

Herniated disc

Can cause similar back and leg pain by compressing a nerve.

Your first evaluation

How is spondylolisthesis
diagnosed?

Diagnosing spondylolisthesis combines your medical history, a neurological exam and imaging. Bring any imaging you already have.

See the clinic
  1. 01

    History and exam

    How long you’ve had pain, what makes it worse and whether it goes down your legs, plus a check of strength, reflexes and sensation.

  2. 02

    Standing X-rays

    Show the slip and its grade; bending forward and back reveals any abnormal movement.

  3. 03

    MRI

    Shows whether nerves are compressed when there is leg pain or weakness.

!

Spondylolisthesis warning signs

Go to an emergency room right away if you lose bladder or bowel control, have numbness in the genitals or between the legs, have severe or worsening weakness in the legs or arms, or have new difficulty walking or using your hands.

A plan for your case

Spondylolisthesis
treatment in Tijuana

Most people with spondylolisthesis are treated without surgery first: medication, physical therapy and, in selected cases, injections. Surgery is considered if the pain doesn’t improve, nerve symptoms persist or the slip is progressing.

01 · Nonsurgical

Medical treatment

Pain and inflammation control with an individual plan and follow-up. Medications are reviewed with your doctor; avoid self-medicating.

Care and services
02 · Movement

Physical therapy

Exercises to strengthen your core and back, improve stability and take load off the affected area.

Care and services
03 · Injections

Epidural injections

An injection near the irritated nerve to relieve leg pain in selected cases and make physical therapy easier.

Care and services
04 · Spine surgery

Decompression

Frees compressed nerves when narrowing is the main problem and the spine is still stable.

Learn about spine surgery
05 · Spine surgery

Spinal fusion

Joins and stabilizes the vertebrae with implants when there is instability or a slip that causes persistent symptoms.

Learn about spine surgery
06 · Spine surgery

Minimally invasive

Some decompressions and fusions can be done through small incisions. Whether that’s an option depends on your anatomy and diagnosis.

Learn about spine surgery

These options aren’t right for everyone. Your consultation covers the goals, alternatives and risks of each one.

Contact the clinic
01

Does spondylolisthesis always need surgery?

No. Most people improve with medication and physical therapy. Surgery is considered when pain persists despite treatment, when there are nerve symptoms or when the slip is progressing.

02

Can the vertebra keep slipping?

In many adults the slip stays stable for years. Follow-up X-rays help monitor it if your symptoms change.

03

What exercises can I do with spondylolisthesis?

Core and back strengthening exercises usually help, but a professional should prescribe them based on your grade and symptoms. Avoid heavy lifting or arching your back without guidance.

04

What tests do I need for my visit?

Bring any X-rays or MRIs you already have. If needed, standing or bending X-rays are ordered to measure the slip.

05

When should I go to the emergency room?

Go right away if you notice changes in bladder or bowel control, loss of feeling in the genitals or between the legs, severe or worsening weakness in the legs or arms, or new difficulty walking or using your hands.
